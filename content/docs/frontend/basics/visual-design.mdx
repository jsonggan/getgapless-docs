---
title: Visual design
description: How layout, spacing, type, and color tell users where to look and what matters.
---

Users do not read a screen. They scan it. Visual design decides what they notice first, what they skip, and what they believe they can click.

## Hierarchy

The most important thing on the screen should be the most noticeable. You control this with size, weight, color, and position.

- Give each screen one primary action. If everything is bold, nothing is.
- Put the most important content where the eye lands first: top left for left-to-right languages, and the center of the screen.
- Make secondary controls smaller and quieter.

In a chat app, the answer is the content. Controls like copy, regenerate, and feedback stay small and muted until the user needs them.

A quick test: blur your eyes or step back from the screen. The element that still stands out should be the one that matters most.

## Spacing

Space groups things. Items placed close together read as related. Items placed apart read as separate.

- Keep related items close, such as a label and its field, or a message and its actions.
- Add more space between unrelated groups than within them.
- Line items up along shared edges. Elements that almost line up look like a mistake.
- Use a small set of spacing sizes and reuse them everywhere. Random spacing makes a screen feel messy even when the content is simple.

A crowded screen feels hard to use. Empty space is not wasted space.

## Typography

- Use one or two typefaces and three or four text sizes. More than that looks chaotic.
- Keep long text at a comfortable line length, around 60 to 80 characters per line. AI answers are often long, so reading comfort matters more than usual.
- Give lines enough height so paragraphs do not feel cramped.
- Use weight and size for headings, not all capitals or underlines.

## Color and contrast

- Use color for meaning, not decoration. One accent color for primary actions, red for destructive actions and errors, green for success.
- Never rely on color alone. About 1 in 12 men has some form of color blindness. Pair color with an icon or a word, such as a red icon plus the text "Failed".
- Keep text readable against its background. The common accessibility standard asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text.
- Check both light and dark mode if you support them. A color that works on white can disappear on black.

## Consistency

The same thing should look and behave the same everywhere. If a blue button means "do this" on one screen, it means the same on every screen.

Consistency lets users learn your product once. Most teams get it by using a design system or component library instead of styling each screen from scratch.
